How does a local rule about change determine an entire trajectory?

We will connect a rate equation to its solution, interpret the time scale, and check what happens when the rate vanishes. The only prerequisites are differentiation, integration, and the exponential function.

1. State the local rule

Let x(t) be a positive quantity. Suppose its rate of decrease is proportional to its current value, with a constant rate k ≥ 0. We choose units so that x(0) = 1.

dxdt=−kx,x(0)=1

This assumption fixes the fractional rate of change. It does not say that the same absolute amount disappears in every interval.

2. Recover the trajectory

On an interval where x is positive, divide the differential equation by x. The left side is the derivative of log x. Integrating from 0 to t gives

logx(t)−logx(0)=−kt.

The initial value makes the second logarithm zero. Exponentiation then produces a positive solution for every finite t:

x(t)=e−kt.

A check that uses the original equation

Differentiate the result: its derivative is −ke−kt, which is −kx(t). The solution equals 1 at t = 0. Both the rate rule and the initial condition hold.

For k > 0, the time scale is 1/k: after that time the quantity is e−1 of its initial value. At k = 0 the solution is constant, so this finite time scale no longer applies.

4. Try a variation

  1. For k > 0, find the time at which the quantity reaches half its initial value. What changes when k = 0?
  2. Change the initial value to a positive number a. Derive the trajectory and decide whether the half-life changes.
  3. Euler's method gives xn+1 = (1 − kh)xn for a step h > 0. For k > 0, when do nonnegative iterates decrease without changing sign? When do they converge to zero even if their signs alternate?
Hint: separate the physical condition from numerical stability

For the last question, examine the multiplier 1 − kh. Nonnegative decay and convergence depend on different conditions on that multiplier.

Worked solutions

1. Solving e−kt = 1/2 gives t = log 2/k. When k = 0, the value remains 1 and never reaches 1/2.

2. The solution is ae−kt. Setting it equal to a/2 cancels a, so the half-life is unchanged for k > 0.

3. Nonnegative monotone decay occurs for 0 < kh ≤ 1. Convergence to zero requires |1 − kh| < 1, or 0 < kh < 2. In the interval 1 < kh < 2 the iterates alternate signs, which is inconsistent with a positive decaying quantity even though the numerical sequence converges.
